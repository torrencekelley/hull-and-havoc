VERSION 1.100  ·  A starship tycoon modelled on Detroit, the classic automobile-company game: run the firm week by week from your headquarters — design in the Design Office, build on crewed assembly lines, sell through dealerships you plant across the galaxy, hire your department heads, and research the next era's technology before your rivals do. Every ship design still carries the house version system: born v1.100, +0.001 at every re-save under the same name.

How to play — the Detroit loop, in space
  • One week per turn. Your charter runs 2708–2728. Each End Week, the week closes: lines build, convoys land, dealerships sell, wages and bills go out, rivals report, and you get the Weekly Report — sales by model, expenses, market share, and the newspaper headline.
  • Design Office: pick size, hull, engines, weapons — then set your own asking price, like Detroit. Price against build cost and market value: greedy prices sit unsold, sharp prices fly off the showroom floor. Test-fly a design (₡150) and the trade press will rate it; a good review raises its appeal — re-save to lock the tested spec in as a new version.
  • Assembly: each yard runs assembly lines tooled for one size class. Crew size and wages are yours to set — bigger crews build faster, better-paid crews turn out higher-quality ships that sell for more, and every crew is a weekly wage bill.
  • Sales & Marketing: ships don't sell themselves. Open dealerships in sectors, stock them by shipping finished ships there, and set a weekly advertising budget per sector. Dealerships sell from stock every week at your asking price; no dealership means only passing brokers, at a trickle. A broker will always take a ship off your hands instantly — at 85% of asking.
  • Personnel: hire department heads — Chief Designer, Yard Foreman, Sales Director, Research Lead. Skill is rated in stars and paid weekly. Good people quietly bend every number in the firm your way.
  • Research: the galaxy's technology marches on by year, Detroit-style. Fund the lab weekly to earn research points, then unlock hulls, drives, and weapons as their era arrives — a heavy lance battery in 2708 is science fiction; by 2713 it's a product line.
  • Rivals: Vossk Combine Yards, the Imperium State Arsenals, and Meridian Starworks sell against you every week. The Boardroom tracks market share — take theirs.
  • Contracts remain the fast money: fleet buyers post orders with deadlines, paid at a premium on delivery.
  • The Galactic Expo: every 52 weeks the year's designs — yours and the rivals' — are judged for Best Warship, Best Freighter, and Best Value. Trophies raise your reputation, and reputation moves the prices buyers will accept.
  • Scout reports: in the Boardroom, pay ₡400 to lift a rival's current spec sheet — what they're building and what they're charging — for 26 weeks. Price with your eyes open.
  • Patents: the first firm to finish a tech patents it. Your patents pay ₡9/week each in rival licensing fees; a tech a rival patented first costs you +50% RP to work around. The tree is a race.
  • Refits: any ship in stock can be refitted with researched engines and weapons for 40% of the new build cost — the refit bumps the ship to a new version and keeps early hulls earning in later eras (and gives frontier buyers an affordable upgrade path).
  • The Loan Desk: in the Boardroom's Office panel, borrow to expand — payments and their interest portion are itemised in the Weekly Report, with the balance and schedule always visible.
  • The timeline: history has a shape. The Boardroom lists the named eras ahead — the Founding War, the Long Quiet (two years when only cheap hulls sell), the Ember Embargo — so you can stock, price, and research around what's coming. Random wars and surges still happen on top.
  • Goal: grow net worth to ₡60,000 before the charter review in 2728. If credits fall below −₡1,000, the creditors take the yards.

    Crews & Wages

    Every yard crew is set on its own card in Assembly — headcount and pay grade together decide the yard's character:

    • Under 30 crew: lines crawl at 60% pace. Cheap, slow, and it shows.
    • 30–70 crew: standard pace.
    • Over 70 crew: double-shift pace, 135% — at double-shift wages.
    • Low pay (₡6): quality 93% — ships sell for less and buyers know why.
    • Standard pay (₡9): quality 100%.
    • High pay (₡13): quality 108% — the yard's name goes on every hull, proudly.

    Quality is stamped onto each ship as it rolls off the line and follows it to the showroom. A good Yard Foreman speeds every line and adds a little quality of his own.
